When Crime Falls but Repression Rises: Migration Responses to Competing Risks

Abstract

How do households respond to changes in security risk, and for whom do these changes alter migration decisions? This paper examines migration responses to El Salvador’s 2022 State of Exception, a large-scale shift in the country’s security environment. I combine nationally representative data on household migration and characteristics with municipality-level violence measures, leaked State of Exception arrest data, and public opinion polling data on perceptions of insecurity to explore this relationship. While reductions in criminal violence may reduce migration incentives, expanded state enforcement may generate new risks. These risks may differ by income: higher-income households may respond to the threat of high-profile arrests, while lower-income households may face greater exposure to broad enforcement and arrests based on perceived gang association. The results show substantial heterogeneity in migration responses, suggesting that households respond not simply to whether security improves, but to how their expected risk changes and their capacity to respond. As governments consider El Salvador’s State of Exception as a model for security policy, these findings highlight an important tradeoff: improvements in public safety may alter migration incentives differently across households depending on how the policy changes their exposure to both criminal and state-related risks.

Migration Shocks and Informal Labor: Gendered Evidence from Post-Pandemic Peru

Abstract

This paper examines how Venezuelan migration affected the labor market outcomes of Peruvian women, with a focus on adjustment in the informal sector before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Rather than treating the post-pandemic period as merely a longer time horizon, I use it to examine whether the labor market margin through which migration affects natives changes over time. Using nationally representative household survey data and a gender-specific shift-share instrument, I estimate the causal effects of migration on labor force participation, informal employment, and labor income. I find that Venezuelan migration reduced labor force participation among Peruvian women while increasing labor income among those who remained employed in the informal sector. These results attenuate by 2022. My findings suggest that migration altered the composition of women's participation in the informal labor market, highlighting the importance of considering multiple margins of labor market adjustment when evaluating the distributional consequences of migration for women in highly informal economies, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic.

When Women Wait: Intrahousehold Migration Decisions

Abstract

A growing literature examines how temporary protection policies affect legal status and labor market outcomes, but much less is known about how they influence migration decisions within households. This paper studies Peru's 2017 Permiso Temporal de Permanencia (PTP), which granted eligible Venezuelans temporary legal status and work authorization, to examine whether legal pathways altered the timing of household migration. Using nationally representative surveys of Venezuelan migrants in Peru, I reconstruct household migration histories and estimate the effect of PTP eligibility on the interval between partners' arrivals. I find that the PTP increased the time between partners' migration, suggesting that households strategically delayed reunification in anticipation of legal migration opportunities. I show that female spouses in particular are responding to the monopsony power of their husbands' employers. This delay in migration also affects children's school enrollment. These findings demonstrate that legal pathways influence not only where migrants move, but also when households migrate.

Household Economics of Immigration Detention: Evidence from U.S. Bond Issuances

Abstract

Immigration detention removes workers from households and local labor markets, but its economic costs are largely unknown. I exploit a U.S. court ruling that sharply reduced eligibility for bond among detained immigrants to estimate the economic effects of detention. Using the ruling's differential impact across individuals, I find that increased detention reduces household employment and income and generates spillovers to local industries with greater exposure to affected workers. Effects are concentrated among households with fewer alternative earners and industries more reliant on immigrant labor. The results show that immigration enforcement generates substantial economic costs beyond the individuals directly subject to detention, affecting household labor supply and local production.
