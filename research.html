RESEARCH

Migration, labor markets, and economic opportunity

My research lies at the intersection of labor economics, development economics, and the economics of migration, with a regional focus on the United States and Latin America. I use applied microeconomic methods and administrative, survey, and novel data to study how policy and institutions affect people's economic choices.

When Crime Falls but Repression Rises: Migration Responses to Competing Risks

Draft available upon request.

Abstract

How do households respond to changes in security risk, and for whom do these changes alter migration decisions? This paper examines migration responses to El Salvador's 2022 State of Exception, a large-scale shift in the country's security environment. I combine nationally representative data on household migration and characteristics with municipality-level violence measures, leaked State of Exception arrest data, and public opinion polling data on perceptions of insecurity to explore this relationship.

While reductions in criminal violence may reduce migration incentives, expanded state enforcement may generate new risks. The results show substantial heterogeneity in migration responses, suggesting that households respond not simply to whether security improves, but to how their expected risk changes and their capacity to respond. As governments consider El Salvador's State of Exception as a model for security policy, these findings highlight an important tradeoff: improvements in public safety may alter migration incentives differently across households depending on how the policy changes their exposure to both criminal and state-related risks.

Migration Shocks and Informal Labor: Gendered Evidence from Post-Pandemic Peru

Draft available upon request.

Abstract

This paper examines how Venezuelan migration affected the labor market outcomes of Peruvian women, with a focus on adjustment in the informal sector before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Rather than treating the post-pandemic period as merely a longer time horizon, I use it to examine whether the labor market margins through which migration affects native workers change over time.

Using nationally representative household survey data and a gender-specific shift-share instrument, I estimate the causal effects of migration on labor force participation, informal employment, and labor income. I find that Venezuelan migration reduced labor force participation among Peruvian women while increasing labor income among those who remained employed in the informal sector. These results attenuate by 2022.

My findings suggest that migration altered the composition of women's participation in the informal labor market, highlighting the importance of considering multiple margins of labor market adjustment when evaluating the distributional consequences of migration for women in highly informal economies, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic.

Household Economics of Immigration Detention: Evidence from U.S. Bond Issuances

Draft available upon request.

Abstract

Immigration detention removes workers from households and local labor markets, but its economic costs are largely unknown. I exploit a U.S. court ruling that sharply reduced eligibility for bond among detained immigrants to estimate the economic effects of detention.

Using the ruling's differential impact across individuals, I find that increased detention reduces household employment and income and generates spillovers to local industries with greater exposure to affected workers. Effects are concentrated among households with fewer alternative earners and industries more reliant on immigrant labor.

The results show that immigration enforcement generates substantial economic costs beyond the individuals directly subject to detention, affecting household labor supply and local production.

Shared Moments, Shifting Beliefs: Evidence from Colombia's Lost Children

With Ignacio González , María Esther Caballero , and Daniela Escobar López

Draft available upon request.

Abstract

We study how a highly salient national event shapes perceptions of a minority group. Our setting is the 2023 rescue of the four Indigenous children who survived for forty days in the Colombian Amazon following a plane crash. The rescue received extensive media coverage in Colombia and abroad, and media accounts emphasized the role of Indigenous people in locating and rescuing the children. Using variation in interview timing around the rescue in a nationally representative survey, we estimate the effect of the event on views toward Indigenous communities. Respondents interviewed after the rescue expressed significantly more favorable views, especially among those who identify as right-wing, whose pre-existing views left greater room for updating. We also find evidence of increased Indigenous self-identification, even though ethnic identities are generally regarded as relatively stable. More broadly, our findings highlight the role of salient collective experiences in shaping social beliefs and identities.
